IntroductionWho I Am and What This Is

I am Bernat Subira, from Barcelona, a senior at FIU completing a double major in International Business and Management.

My purpose is straightforward: work for a multinational company, or own my own business. International Business gives me the breadth — marketing, finance, logistics, operations, strategy, applied across borders. Management gives me the leadership layer on top. Together they prepare me for either route, which is exactly why I chose both.

I am also co-founder and CMO of Milonga, a Miami functional beverage brand, where I own the entire marketing function. That means this course was never theoretical for me. It gave me language and structure for things I was already doing by instinct — and, more usefully, showed me where instinct had been quietly failing.

This portfolio runs week by week and covers every module assignment and discussion board from the semester. For each: what it was for, a sample of what I produced, and what I actually took from it. Where an assignment changed how I work, I say so. Where it didn't, I say that too.

W1

Week 1Self-Awareness and Career Direction

Purpose. Diagnostic week. Before you can build a career plan you need two things: a defensible reason for the field you chose, and an accurate read on how you actually operate. The syllabus quiz set the ground rules; the essay and the assessment supplied those two inputs.

Syllabus Quiz. Easy to dismiss as administrative. It isn't — it establishes that deadlines, formatting, and academic integrity standards are non-negotiable, which is the same contract that governs professional work. Reading the rules before starting is itself an executive habit.

Career Goal Essay — sample. I argued that choosing International Business and Management was both personal and strategic. Personal: it was my father's field, and I am drawn to languages and cross-cultural work. Strategic: International Business touches nearly every business function, and Management supplies the leadership layer. My conclusion was that the combination makes me versatile — the trait that matters most for someone aiming at building or leading a company rather than filling a narrow functional seat.

Personality Assessment Career Reflection — sample. The assessment returned a profile built around strategic thinking, long-horizon planning, and a strong drive to lead and build systems. The flattering parts were easy to accept. The useful part was the shadow side: perfectionism, difficulty delegating, sensitivity to criticism, and a tendency to avoid conflict.

Discussion board — Introduce Yourself.

My name is Bernat Subira. I am currently majoring in International Business and Management, and I am passionate about business, leadership, and innovation. My long-term career goal is to become either an entrepreneur by building and growing my own business or an intrapreneur within a multinational company, where I can lead innovative projects, solve business challenges, and create value on a global scale. From this Executive Skills course, I hope to develop the professional and leadership skills that are essential in today's business environment — communication, teamwork, critical thinking, problem-solving, decision-making, and leadership. I am from Barcelona, Spain, and I enjoy learning new things and continuously improving myself.

Discussion board — Top Soft Skills. I identified adaptability, communication, and continuous learning as the three I rely on most, and argued that adaptability is the one that makes the other two durable — skills learned now expire, but the habit of relearning doesn't.

Discussion board — Personality Avatar. I shared the avatar generated by my assessment and explained which traits I recognized immediately and which I resisted. The ones I resisted turned out to be the accurate ones.

What I learned, and was it helpful. The essay mostly confirmed what I already believed — the real payoff came later, in the mock interview, when "why this major?" came out clean because I had already argued it in writing.

The assessment was the genuinely valuable piece. Being shown that my perfectionism and over-thinking are predictable outputs of how I'm wired, not personal failures, reframed them as things to design around rather than feel guilty about. My response was structural: reversible decisions now get a deadline measured in hours, not days. That rule came out of Week 1 and I still use it.

Week 2Career Target, Development Plan, and Self-Marketing

Purpose. Move from who am I to what am I aiming at, what's missing, and how do I read to a stranger. Four assignments, each handling one of those.

Dream Job Description Analysis — sample. I chose International Business Development Manager at a multinational: identifying new international markets, building client and partner relationships, developing market-entry strategy, negotiating deals, and coordinating cross-border teams. I chose it because it sits exactly where my two majors meet, and because it is a credible bridge role — it builds the deal-making and cross-functional leadership that an entrepreneur or intrapreneur eventually needs.

Personal Development plan — sample. Analyzing that job description against my current profile produced a concrete gap list rather than a vague ambition:

  • Practical experience through internships and entry-level work
  • Leadership and project management reps
  • Public speaking, negotiation, and presentation ability
  • Deeper grounding in marketing, sales, logistics, finance, and data analysis
  • Continued language development — multilingualism is a real differentiator in international business
  • Fluency with AI and digital business tools, which is now assumed rather than distinguishing

The plan against it: pursue internships while finishing the degree, attend career fairs, network deliberately, build the LinkedIn presence, and start in an entry-level business role after graduation before moving toward greater responsibility — with continuous certification and mentorship throughout.

LinkedIn Profile. I rebuilt my profile around a single positioning statement rather than a list of activities. The test I used: could a recruiter tell what I do and what I'm looking for from the headline alone, without scrolling?

"Blurb" Video. Covered in the Projects section below.

Discussion board — Personal SWOT Analysis.

Strengths. My knowledge in international business and management, which lets me understand how companies work in domestic and international markets. Fluency in several languages, which enables me to interact with people from different cultures. A passion for learning — I constantly seek knowledge not just in international business but in marketing, entrepreneurship, logistics, sales, and artificial intelligence. A strong work ethic, and I like working with people.

Weaknesses. I need practical experience through internships and work experience. I am a perfectionist and an over-thinker, and I set high standards that lead to self-imposed pressure. I get critical of myself rather than optimistic about what I've accomplished. Networking needs improvement — I like making friends, but I understand networking is different and benefits me long term. Leadership and public speaking also need development.

Opportunities. Globalization and the growth of multinationals create real opportunities in my field. Internships, networking events, certifications, and professional associations are all available to me. My interests across marketing, logistics, entrepreneurship, and AI let me build a diversified skills portfolio that makes me competitive.

Threats. High competition — many candidates have master's degrees, certifications, or years of experience. Technology and AI are changing how companies run, which means I'll be learning for the rest of my career. Economic instability in global markets can also affect job prospects.

Discussion board — Reflecting on Your LinkedIn Presence. I audited my own profile honestly: it existed, but it was a résumé transcribed into a website. No positioning, no evidence of work, no reason for anyone to reach out. The fix was to lead with what I'm building rather than what I've completed.

What I learned, and was it helpful. The most useful week of the course, for two reasons.

The Dream Job analysis converted an ambition into a specification. "I want to work in international business" is not a plan. "International Business Development Manager, six named competencies, I currently hold three" is. You cannot close a gap you have not measured.

And the SWOT forced me to write down the thing I had been avoiding: networking is my weakness, and it's the one that compounds. Experience, leadership reps, public speaking — networking partially solves all of them. Naming it as the highest-leverage weakness instead of one item on a list changed my priorities.

W3

Week 3Leadership, Mentorship, and Professional Networks

Purpose. Study how a real leader operates rather than how leadership is described in a textbook, and identify the structures that connect me to my target industry.

Executive Profile & Personal Reflection — sample. I profiled Alex Hormozi, chosen because he documents the reasoning behind his decisions rather than only the outcomes, which makes him usable as a virtual mentor rather than just a case study.

What I took from him is not the tactics. It's that his central discipline is removing himself as the bottleneck — defining a standard precisely enough that someone else can hit it, then getting out of the way. Everything else he does is downstream of that one habit.

My reflection was uncomfortable to write, because that is precisely the skill my Week 1 weaknesses block. Perfectionism says nobody else will do it to standard. Difficulty delegating says it's faster if I do it. Both are wrong at any scale beyond one person, and both are why I have shipped elaborate systems while simpler high-leverage work sat untouched.

Connecting with Your Future: Professional Associations — sample. I researched the Academy of International Business (AIB), founded in 1959, spanning scholars, students, and practitioners across more than 90 countries. Membership offers four concrete things: access to professionals, professors, and researchers in my exact field; educational resources across global marketing, trade, FDI, international finance, supply chain, and cross-cultural management; conferences and regional events where relationships are built in person and presentation skills get real reps; and a résumé line that signals active commitment to the profession rather than passive interest.

Discussion board — To Network or Not Network.

I believe networking is a very useful tool for personal and professional growth, even though it can feel uncomfortable at first. Building relationships with people who share similar interests creates valuable opportunities — learning from others, finding career opportunities, and gaining different perspectives. While networking events may feel awkward, especially when starting conversations with strangers, the benefits outweigh the discomfort. Developing communication skills and becoming comfortable speaking with new people is important for success in business. Networking is especially important for students, because the connections we build today can help us in the future. Online communication is convenient, but face-to-face interactions allow people to build stronger and more meaningful relationships.

Discussion board — Coaching vs. Mentoring.

Coaching and mentoring are both forms of employee development, but they work differently. Coaching develops specific skills and enhances job performance through feedback, training, and goal setting. The benefits are better performance, faster skill development, and more confidence. Mentoring is a long-term relationship in which an experienced professional offers guidance, career advice, and support. It focuses on individual and career development, leadership development, and helping employees adapt to the organization. Both matter, because coaching helps employees succeed in their current jobs while mentoring prepares them for future opportunities. Together they contribute to long-term professional success.

What I learned, and was it helpful. Helpful, with one uncomfortable finding. I wrote a well-reasoned post about the value of networking — and I have joined zero professional associations. The gap between what I can argue and what I have done is the honest result of Week 3.

The coaching/mentoring distinction changed something concrete. I had been treating every advisory relationship identically. Now I separate them: a skill problem gets a coach and a narrow question; a direction problem gets a mentor and a wide one. Asking a mentor to debug tactics wastes their time and mine.

Week 4Goal Setting and Time Management

Purpose. Close the gap between intention and action, and stop confusing urgent with important.

SMART Goal Setting — sample.

Goal Measure Deadline
Graduate FIU with the double major complete GPA of 3.7 or higher Dec 15, 2026
Help scale Milonga into a defined leadership role Revenue targets met; marketing functions documented as SOPs another person can run Dec 15, 2026
Build the foundation for my own venture A completed business plan plus two business/marketing certifications Jun 30, 2027

Eisenhower Matrix — sample.

Urgent Not urgent
Important DO — graded coursework due this week; live campaign launches; anything blocking another person's work DECIDE — internship applications and outreach; building transferable SOPs; public speaking and negotiation reps; training; long-horizon strategy
Not important DELEGATE — content post-production and compositing; routine scheduling and posting; repetitive admin DELETE — passive scrolling; over-polishing work already past the quality threshold; re-checking finished work

Discussion board — Staying Focused and Following Through.

One thing that helps me stay focused on my goals is having a personal dashboard where I track all of my goals and the daily habits I need to complete. Each habit is connected to one of my goals, so I know that if I complete my habits consistently, I'm making progress toward achieving them. I also try to eliminate distractions while I'm working and stay consistent instead of waiting to feel motivated. My recommendation for others is to build habits that support your goals and focus on completing those habits every day. Small, consistent actions lead to big results over time.

What I learned, and was it helpful. Helpful, and it exposed a real flaw in a system I already ran. I was tracking goals and habits — but my goals weren't written to SMART standard. "Grow Milonga" sat on my dashboard for months. It isn't measurable, so it can never be checked off, so it produced guilt instead of progress. Adding a revenue threshold and a documentation criterion made it auditable for the first time.

The matrix diagnosed something worse. My schedule was full of Quadrant 1 emergencies and I'd been reading that as productivity. It isn't. A full Quadrant 1 means Quadrant 2 was neglected until it caught fire. Almost every crisis I handle is a Quadrant 2 task I deferred — internship applications being the clearest case: not urgent for months, then urgent and too late.

The quadrant that actually changed my behaviour was DELETE, because most of my wasted time isn't entertainment. It's over-polishing work that was already good enough. That's Week 1's perfectionism reappearing as a scheduling problem.

Week 5Interviewing and Negotiation

Purpose. Performance week. Everything before this was preparation; this tested whether the preparation survives a real conversation about being hired and paid.

Salary & Compensation Research Report — sample. I researched Account Executive (B2B SaaS Sales) as my realistic first role: software sales to organizations, prospecting, client relationship management, contract negotiation, and supporting market entry.

  • Base pay: roughly $99,000 average for entry-level in the US (Salary.com).
  • Total compensation: commission, profit-sharing, incentive pay, and performance bonuses, plus health insurance, PTO, 401(k), and equity at some firms. Top-performing entry-level reps frequently clear $120K–$150K once commission is counted.
  • What moves the number: sales performance dominates, since commission tracks it directly. Then prior sales experience, internships, education, industry, firm size, and location. Leadership experience, CRM fluency (Salesforce), negotiation ability, and multilingualism all raise starting offers — and multinationals and large tech firms pay above smaller companies.
  • Required competencies (O*NET): communication, persuasion, negotiation, relationship-building, problem-solving. Daily work spans prospecting, presentations, needs discovery, contract and price negotiation, account management, and quota. For international accounts: market knowledge, cultural sensitivity, CRM, and data analysis.
  • Progression: Senior AE → Enterprise AE → International Sales Manager → Director of Business Development → VP of Sales.

Partner Project — Mock Interview. Covered in the Projects section below.

Discussion board — Negotiating for a Good Offer of Employment.

I would choose the Amazon offer, because it has a higher starting salary ($65K vs. $55K), 100% employer-paid health insurance, and the chance to move between different analytics teams. That experience improves Nicolas's chances of future promotions and lets him acquire new skills. He also avoids working on Pacific Standard Time, since the role is in Miami. If I were negotiating the offer, I would ask about pay, performance bonuses, opportunities for professional advancement, and additional paid time off. Before negotiating, I would research comparable salaries in the Miami region. Rather than simply asking for more money, an effective negotiation tactic is to present your accomplishments, internships, and skills. Overall, Amazon offers a better combination of pay, professional growth, and work-life balance.

What I learned, and was it helpful. The single most useful week for immediate employability.

The salary research changed how I think about compensation entirely. I'd been treating salary as one number. It isn't — it's base, commission structure, bonus, equity, benefits cost, PTO, and development budget, and several of those are far more negotiable than base. In a commission-driven role, the commission structure matters more than the base, and I wouldn't have known to ask.

The mock interview taught me what the reading couldn't: preparation shows up as speed, not polish. Where I had already written the answer — why this major, what my weaknesses are, what I want long term — the response was immediate and specific. Where I hadn't, I filled space while thinking, and the difference is audible on the recording. Pollak's instruction to over-prepare isn't about memorizing scripts. It's about doing the thinking in advance so the interview is retrieval rather than composition.

ProjectsOn Camera

Two recorded projects, and the two assignments I was most reluctant to do.

"Blurb" Video (Week 2) — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHTC3H6-cz4 A short spoken introduction: who I am, what I do, and what I'm looking for. Essentially an elevator pitch with a camera pointed at it.

Mock Interview (Week 5) — https://youtu.be/T0V2ESQiMDA A partner project in which I played both interviewer and candidate, then exchanged feedback.

What I learned most from these two

Written competence does not transfer automatically to spoken competence. I can write a clear, structured argument. On camera — no editing, no second draft — the same content came out slower and less organized. That's not a knowledge problem, it's a reps problem, and reps are the only fix.

Playback is the fastest feedback mechanism I have access to. Filler words, a habit of explaining background before getting to the point, hedging strong claims — no written feedback surfaces those as efficiently as ninety seconds of watching yourself back.

Playing the interviewer taught me more than being the candidate. From that chair you can see immediately which answers are substantive and which are pleasant noise. Vague answers are obvious to the person asking in a way they never are to the person answering. It made me significantly less tolerant of vagueness in my own.

The two projects tested opposite skills. The Blurb is a monologue — you control the whole frame, so the failure mode is rambling. The interview is reactive — you control nothing, so the failure mode is being caught without a prepared answer. Getting good at one does not make you good at the other.

Did I have fun? The Blurb, yes — once I stopped re-recording. The mock interview, not during; it was uncomfortable, and the first playback was worse. But it was the most professionally realistic thing I did in this course, and it's the assignment I'd keep if everything else were cut. I would rather be uncomfortable in a mock interview than unprepared in a real one.

Something ExtraBuilding What I'm Studying

The strongest evidence of a skill is a working artifact, not an essay about the skill. As co-founder and CMO of Milonga, I built the brand's entire marketing operating system:

  • Brand foundation — identity from zero: positioning across three product lines, four defined customer personas, a narrative framework, a controlled brand vocabulary, and the guidelines document our graphic designer works from.
  • Production pipeline — a repeatable process taking a concept from first image through animation to finished composite, with fixed production rules so output stays consistent regardless of who runs it. It has produced video used in retail placement.
  • Analytics and reporting — weekly, monthly, and quarterly reporting cadences pulling live e-commerce, email, and social data, so strategy decisions run on numbers instead of opinion.
  • Creator and affiliate program — brief, outreach, onboarding, CRM, and a retention ladder, built as a documented pipeline rather than ad-hoc effort.
  • Delegation infrastructure — every one of the above documented so someone other than me can run it.

Why this belongs in an executive skills portfolio. Each item maps to a course competency. Brand architecture and personas are strategic decision-making. Guidelines and SOPs are delegation and leadership — you cannot delegate what you have not defined. Reporting cadences are SMART goals applied to a business instead of a person. Creator and retail conversations are negotiation. And building systems instead of doing tasks is the Eisenhower Matrix as an operating philosophy: my Quadrant 2 work is precisely what empties my Quadrant 1.

Free-writing — the topic this course didn't cover, and should have

One subject sat underneath this entire course without being named: AI fluency is now a baseline executive skill, not a specialization.

Several assignments touched its edges. My Dream Job analysis listed AI and digital tools as a competency to develop. My SWOT listed technological change as a threat. But the course treated it as a topic to be aware of rather than a capability to build, and I think that framing is already out of date.

Here's what I've observed running an actual marketing function: the advantage isn't going to people who know AI exists. It's going to people who can decompose their own work precisely enough to hand parts of it off — to a tool, a contractor, or a junior hire — and then verify the output against a standard.

That is not a technology skill. It's a management skill, and it's exactly what this course already teaches under different names: define the outcome, specify the standard, delegate the execution, inspect the result. In my experience, people who struggle to delegate to AI are the same people who struggle to delegate to humans, for the same reason — they have never made their own quality standards explicit enough to hand to anyone.

Which is the conclusion I'm taking out of this course. These are billed as career-preparation skills, and they are. But they're also operating skills, and they work at the level of a person, a team, or a company largely unchanged. A SMART goal works on a career or a revenue target. An Eisenhower Matrix sorts a student's week or a department's roadmap. Over-preparation wins an interview or a buyer meeting. That transferability is what makes them worth keeping long after the grade is recorded.

Next

Forward Plan

My weaknesses showed up consistently across every assignment that surfaced them, which tells me they're structural rather than situational: limited practical experience, underdeveloped networking, perfectionism that converts into delay, and difficulty delegating.

  1. Experience — five qualified applications a week, every career fair, two informational interviews a month.
  2. Networking — join AIB and treat attendance as non-negotiable rather than aspirational. Fifteen deliberate new contacts a month, each with a personal message.
  3. Perfectionism — enforce the reversible-decision deadline. Ship at the threshold, not past it, and move the recovered hours into Quadrant 2.
  4. Delegation — keep converting my own tasks into documented processes. It's the only thing that scales past my personal capacity.
  5. Speaking — keep recording myself. Playback is the cheapest correction loop I have.

I came into this course able to describe what I wanted. I'm leaving with the described version replaced by a scheduled one — which is, I think, the entire point.
